Key Points from Part 2 of the Recording
1. Innovation is a process from ideas to market value
The lecturer frames innovation as a progression:
1.	Generate ideas or insights.
2.	Identify a problem.
3.	Develop one or more solutions.
4.	Refine and test the solution.
5.	Create a prototype or pilot.
6.	Define a business model.
7.	Launch into a selected market segment.
8.	Gather feedback and improve further.
Innovation is therefore not just having a “wow” idea or making an invention. A promising idea must be developed, validated, organized commercially, and tested with real users before it can become a successful innovation. 1 2
Example:
Suppose someone designs a smart water bottle that reminds users to drink water. The initial concept is an idea. Building a working bottle is an invention or prototype. It becomes innovation only when the team identifies target customers, sets a price, develops customer support, tests it with users, and improves the product based on feedback.
 
2. Innovation is incremental as a process—but that is different from incremental innovation
A major distinction in the lecture is:
•	Innovation as a process is usually incremental: it develops step by step through experimentation, iteration, pilots, feedback, and improvement.
•	Incremental innovation is a specific type of innovation involving relatively small changes to existing products, components, or systems.
These ideas should not be confused. Even a radical product may be developed through many small, sequential stages. 2
Example:
Creating a completely new electric aircraft may be radical innovation. Yet its development will still be incremental: early designs, prototype testing, safety trials, battery improvements, regulatory approval, and limited market launch.
 
3. Why study innovation theory?
The lecturer argues that innovation theories are useful for more than passing a textbook-based course. They help students understand:
•	how technologies have evolved historically;
•	recurring patterns in technological change;
•	possible future opportunities and risks;
•	how data and past trends can support monitoring, governance, and prediction;
•	how to make more informed decisions when launching entrepreneurial products or services. 2
However, historical patterns are guides, not exact forecasts. The future will not necessarily repeat the past in the same form.
Example:
Studying the shift from mainframe computers to personal computers and smartphones can help managers recognize that technological capability often becomes cheaper, more accessible, and more widely distributed over time. It does not guarantee that every current technology will follow precisely the same path, but it creates strategic awareness. 6
 
4. Technology trajectories can become evolutionary traps
A technology trajectory is the path of development shaped by past experience, decisions, and previous successes. The lecturer compares it to following a railway track: organizations often continue along a familiar route because it worked before. 2
The danger is an evolutionary trap. This occurs when a firm applies the same decisions or strategies that previously succeeded, even though the external environment has changed. The company becomes overly attached to the old path and fails to reconsider, redesign, or deviate when needed. 3
Example:
A company that succeeded by selling high-priced desktop software may keep improving that model while customers shift toward cloud-based subscriptions and mobile access. Its past success may become a trap if it prevents the firm from changing its architecture, pricing, or delivery model.
 
5. A simple framework: steady versus radical/disruptive change
The lecture initially groups innovation into two broad camps:
Steady / incremental side	Radical / disruptive side
Gradual, safer, step-by-step change	Fundamental difference from previous practice
Consolidates existing strengths	Challenges or replaces existing ways of operating

The lecturer notes that actual innovations can combine features of both camps, but the distinction is useful for thinking strategically. 3
Example:
Improving a phone’s battery life by 10% is incremental. Replacing the conventional smartphone interface with a radically different wearable computing system would be closer to radical innovation.

6. Incremental innovation
Incremental innovation makes relatively small improvements to existing components and relationships. It is usually safer, easier to measure, and easier to justify internally because firms may have data showing cost savings, speed improvements, or quality gains. 3 5
Example:
A manufacturer reduces production time by 1%, lowers material waste, or improves battery life slightly without changing the product’s basic design.
Strategic caution:
The lecturer warns that organizations can become too dependent on incrementalism. Small continuous improvements may be insufficient when global technological change is rapid and more fundamental breakthroughs are needed. 5
 
7. Modular innovation
Modular innovation changes or introduces a major component while leaving the overall architecture relatively stable. It can improve a product’s versatility, functionality, or robustness. 3 4
Examples from the lecture:
•	A USB device can serve different functions: storage, Wi-Fi transmission, Bluetooth reception, security authentication, or as a dongle.
•	A hand-powered or solar-powered radio replaces reliance on disposable batteries with a new energy-related module. 4
Simple example:
Adding a high-quality camera module to an existing smartphone platform is modular innovation if the rest of the phone’s overall architecture remains largely unchanged.
 
8. Architectural innovation
Architectural innovation primarily changes how components are arranged, connected, or integrated. The components may be familiar, but their relationship is redesigned to create significantly different capabilities. 4
Example from the lecture:
Changes to computer motherboards and CPU socket designs can require different components to fit and operate together. This alters the system’s underlying architecture and can enable higher speed or new performance characteristics. 4
Simple example:
A computer may use broadly familiar processors, memory, and storage devices, but a redesigned board architecture allows faster data transfer and supports a new range of compatible hardware.
 
9. Radical innovation
Radical innovation involves substantial changes in both components and architecture. It is risky because the technology may be immature, customers may not understand it, and the organization may have little prior experience on which to rely. 4
The lecture emphasizes that customer acceptance is critical. Even an impressive new technology can fail if customers are skeptical, confused, or afraid to adopt it. 4
Examples:
•	The first automobiles were difficult to sell because people were used to horse-drawn transport and had little experience with cars.
•	Early products such as the Sony Walkman, timer-based rice cookers, face detection in cameras, and internet-enabled phones produced a strong “wow” effect because they were unfamiliar at the time. What counts as radical or innovative depends partly on the historical period and the audience’s prior experience. 4 5
 
10. Radical innovation is not automatically the best strategy
The lecturer does not recommend choosing radical innovation simply because it is more exciting. Successful innovation must be sustainable. Firms may begin with incremental improvement and move toward more radical change only when market conditions, resources, customer readiness, and strategic need support it. 4
Example:
A healthcare company might first improve an existing diagnostic device, build customer trust, and collect evidence of value. It may later introduce a more radical AI-based diagnostic platform when users, regulators, and the market are ready.
 
Creative Destruction vs. Disruptive Innovation
11. Schumpeter’s creative destruction
Creative destruction, associated with Joseph Schumpeter and discussed in the lecture as dating to 1942, describes a broad economic process. New technologies, products, and industries replace older ones, transforming the economy and society. This change can disrupt jobs, companies, and established ways of life, but the theory sees it as a process through which society can eventually advance. 5 6
Example:
A large-scale industrial transformation—such as a shift from an older production system to a new technology-driven economy—can make some businesses obsolete while enabling new industries and capabilities.
 
12. Christensen’s disruptive innovation
The lecturer distinguishes this from Clayton Christensen’s more specific theory of disruptive innovation.
In Christensen’s definition, disruptive innovation is not simply a breakthrough that makes an already good product better. It makes products or services that were previously expensive, complicated, and restricted to wealthy or highly skilled users more affordable and accessible to a much larger population. 6
Core pattern:
1.	Established firms dominate the profitable, high-end market.
2.	Entrants begin with cheaper, simpler offerings in low-end or overlooked segments.
3.	Incumbents initially ignore them because the low-end market seems less profitable.
4.	Entrants improve their product and move upward in the market.
5.	Eventually, they can challenge or displace established firms. 7
Example from the lecture:
Digital computing moved from expensive mainframes used by major corporations and universities to minicomputers, desktop computers, laptops, and smartphones. Over time, technology became accessible to far more people. 6
 
13. Why incumbents often fail to respond
Incumbent firms may not ignore challengers because they are irrational. Their cost structures, processes, brand positioning, and profit expectations are optimized for high-end customers and high-margin products. Competing in low-cost segments may therefore appear financially unattractive. 8 9
This creates an asymmetry of motivation:
•	The small entrant is strongly motivated to attack an overlooked market.
•	The established firm is often motivated to leave or avoid that market.
Example: Toyota’s rise
Toyota entered the automobile market with lower-end models such as the Corona, then moved upward through the Tercel, Corolla, Camry, Avalon, 4Runner, and eventually Lexus. North American manufacturers often focused instead on higher-margin vehicles, finding low-end competition less attractive. This gave Toyota room to improve, expand, and move upmarket. 8 9
 
14. Low-cost airlines as a disruption example
The lecture uses budget airlines to explain how entrants can target customers that incumbents do not serve effectively. A no-frills airline separates a traditional full-service ticket into a low basic price plus optional paid extras, such as preferred seats, baggage, or other services. This appeals to customers who mainly want affordable transport. 9
Example:
A traveler who only needs a seat and travels with minimal luggage may prefer a budget airline over a full-service carrier. The entrant does not initially need to win every premium customer; it can survive by serving a distinct low-cost segment.
 
15. Disruption can create opportunities
Disruption is not necessarily harmful. It may be uncomfortable because it requires people and firms to adapt, but it can reveal new markets and opportunities. The lecturer encourages students to identify and welcome beneficial changes rather than only viewing them as threats. 9 10
Examples of opportunity drivers:
•	rising personal income;
•	changing health, wellbeing, retirement, fitness, and environmental preferences;
•	advances in neuroscience, smartphones, AI chips, and processes;
•	falling computing costs and increasing computing power, associated with Moore’s Law. 10
 
Open Innovation
16. Closed versus open innovation
Historically, firms often followed a closed innovation model: ideas, people, resources, and development activities remained inside the company, while external knowledge was treated as confidential. The lecture describes this as common from roughly the 1970s to 1990s. 10
However, a closed model has important limitations:
•	no company possesses all necessary expertise;
•	internal resources are limited;
•	firms may lack customer access or market knowledge;
•	innovation can become slower and more expensive. 10
Open innovation means deliberately involving external contributors to varying degrees: customers, experts, partners, universities, research institutes, suppliers, developers, other industries, and even competitors. 10
 
17. Benefits and safeguards of open innovation
Open innovation can provide:
•	access to outside expertise;
•	more ideas;
•	faster development;
•	lower costs;
•	stronger competitiveness;
•	new business-model possibilities;
•	customer co-creation;
•	stronger relationships and trust;
•	participation in a broader innovation ecosystem. 10
However, openness must be managed. The lecturer stresses the importance of intellectual-property protection, non-disclosure agreements, due diligence, and clear agreements about profit sharing. 10 11
Example:
A small medical-device company may license technology from a university, partner with a hospital for clinical testing, collaborate with a manufacturer for production, and co-design features with patients. Each participant contributes capabilities that the firm could not easily build alone.
 
18. Open innovation allows good ideas to travel further
In a closed innovation funnel, potentially valuable ideas may stop because the company does not have the resources, expertise, or strategic fit to develop them. In an open model, the organization can license, transfer, or partner with external parties that can develop those ideas further. This can generate additional revenue and increase the chance that useful ideas succeed. 11
Example:
A company invents a technology that does not fit its main product line. Instead of abandoning it, the company licenses it to another firm that has the appropriate market access and manufacturing capability.
 
19. NVIDIA and CUDA as an open-innovation ecosystem example
The lecture presents NVIDIA’s CUDA platform as an example of combining a hardware business with an ecosystem strategy. NVIDIA began as a graphics-processing company and later applied GPU capabilities to AI. CUDA provides tools and a platform through which external developers can build applications, share knowledge, access documentation, reuse code, and participate in a developer community. 11
The strategic logic is:
1.	Encourage external developers to create tools and applications.
2.	Build a large community and growing pool of reusable knowledge.
3.	Make the platform increasingly valuable.
4.	Create demand for NVIDIA-compatible hardware, strengthening customer lock-in. 11
Example:
A startup may choose CUDA because it offers existing libraries, tutorials, community support, and compatible development tools. As it builds more of its work around CUDA, moving to another hardware ecosystem becomes more difficult and costly.

•	Innovation moves from ideas to market value through iterative development, prototypes, pilots, business models, testing, and feedback.
•	Do not confuse innovation’s incremental development process with incremental innovation as a specific category.
•	Innovation theory helps firms understand technology trajectories, anticipate patterns, and avoid being trapped by past success.
•	The four innovation types are incremental, modular, architectural, and radical.
•	Radical innovation can be valuable but is riskier; sustainability and customer acceptance matter.
•	Creative destruction concerns broad economic transformation, while Christensen’s disruptive innovation explains how entrants can use simpler, cheaper offerings to challenge incumbents.
